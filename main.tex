\chapter{Background: Information Extraction and Semantics}
\section{Semi-Structured Web Data and Job Postings}
Web-based job postings represent a prime example of semi-structured data. Unlike fully structured databases, these postings are primarily designed for human readability, often embedded within complex HTML structures. This presentation leads to several inherent challenges for automated processing:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Noise and Boilerplate:} Job descriptions are frequently surrounded by non-relevant content, such as "About Us" sections, legal disclaimers, navigation menus, and "Equal Opportunity Employer" statements. Distinguishing the core job requirements from this noise is a critical first step.
    \item \textbf{Layout Variability:} There is no universal standard for how a job post should be structured. Different platforms (e.g., LinkedIn, Indeed, Glassdoor) and individual company career pages use vastly different HTML layouts and CSS classes.
    \item \textbf{Data Volatility and Expiry:} Job postings are ephemeral. Once a position is filled, the page may be removed or return a 404 error, making historical analysis and link consistency difficult.
    \item \textbf{Redundancy and Duplication:} A single internship opportunity is often cross-posted across multiple job boards, sometimes with subtle variations in the title or formatting, necessitating robust deduplication strategies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Information Extraction (IE) Fundamentals}
Information Extraction (IE) is the task of automatically pulling structured information from unstructured or semi-structured documents \cite{sarawagi2008information}. In the recruitment domain, IE aims to convert a block of text into a set of discrete attributes. Key components of IE include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Named Entity Recognition (NER):} The identification of entities such as company names, locations (cities, regions), and dates. For internship posts, this also extends to recognizing specific technologies and tools.
    \item \textbf{Slot Filling and Template Extraction:} This involves populating a predefined schema with specific values extracted from the text, such as salary ranges, duration of the internship, and required degree levels.
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based vs. Statistical Methods:} Traditional IE often relied on hand-crafted regular expressions (Regex) and gazetteers (dictionaries). Modern approaches favor machine learning models, though lightweight rule-based systems remain highly effective for structured patterns like currency and dates.
    \item \textbf{Relation Extraction:} Identifying the semantic links between entities, such as confirming that a specific skill (e.g., "Python") is a "required" qualification rather than just "nice-to-have."
\end{itemize}

\section{Semantic Modeling}
Semantic modeling provides the conceptual framework for organizing extracted data so that it can be queried and interpreted meaningfully by machines.

\subsection{Schemas vs Ontologies vs Taxonomies}
Understanding the distinction between these three concepts is vital for choosing the right level of complexity for a system:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Taxonomies:} Hierarchical classifications that define "is-a" relationships (e.g., "Software Engineer" is a sub-type of "IT Professional"). They are primarily used for categorization and navigation.
    \item \textbf{Ontologies:} The most expressive form, defining not just hierarchies but also complex relationships, properties, and constraints between concepts \cite{gruber1993translation}. They allow for logical reasoning and inference.
    \item \textbf{Schemas:} A structural definition of a data model, specifying fields, types, and constraints (e.g., a JSON or SQL schema).
\end{itemize}
For this thesis, we adopt a \textit{lightweight schema-first approach}. While full ontologies offer great power, they are often too computationally expensive and difficult to maintain for the rapid, high-volume processing required for web-scraped job data.

\subsection{Skill and Occupation Taxonomies}
To avoid a fragmented data space where "Software Dev" and "Programmer" are treated as unrelated, we utilize standardized reference taxonomies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ESCO (European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations):} A multilingual classification system that maps thousands of skills to specific occupations, providing a common language for the labor market \cite{esco_portal}.
    \item \textbf{O*NET (Occupational Information Network):} A comprehensive database developed by the US Department of Labor that defines the "DNA" of occupations, including required knowledge, skills, and abilities \cite{onet_online}.
\end{itemize}
In our pipeline, these taxonomies serve as canonical "anchor points" for mapping raw, noisy text to standardized labels.

\section{Normalization and Canonicalization}
Raw extracted data is rarely ready for end-user consumption or efficient search. Normalization and canonicalization are the processes of bringing data into a consistent format:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Title Normalization:} Standardizing varied strings like "Sr. Software Eng.", "Senior Developer", and "SDE II" into a single canonical form (e.g., "Senior Software Engineer").
    \item \textbf{Synonym and Alias Mapping:} Consolidating variations of the same skill, such as mapping "JS", "ECMAScript", and "Javascript" to the unique identifier "JavaScript".
    \item \textbf{Value Standardization:} Converting heterogeneous data points—such as dates ("2 weeks ago" vs "2023-10-12") and salaries—into ISO-compliant formats and base units.
    \item \textbf{Entity Resolution:} The process of determining whether two different postings refer to the same job opening at the same company, which is essential for accurate analytics.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background: Text Representation and Retrieval}
Search and retrieval are the core mechanisms for accessing internship listings once they have been processed. In this chapter, we explore the evolution from traditional lexical matching to modern semantic search techniques, highlighting the trade-offs in accuracy and performance.

\section{Keyword Retrieval}
Keyword-based retrieval, also known as lexical or sparse retrieval, relies on the presence of exact words or tokens in both the query and the document. This approach has been the foundation of information retrieval for decades.

\subsection{Inverted Indexing}
The fundamental data structure for keyword search is the \textit{inverted index}. It maps each unique term (token) to a list of document identifiers (postings) containing that term. This allows for sub-linear search time regardless of the total number of documents.

\subsection{BM25 (Best Matching 25)}
While simple boolean retrieval checks for term presence, ranking models like BM25 provide a sophisticated scoring mechanism based on probabilistic principles. BM25 improves upon the classic TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) by introducing two key parameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Term Frequency Saturation ($k_1$):} It limits the contribution of a term that appears many times in a single document, preventing "keyword stuffing" from overly influencing the score.
    \item \textbf{Length Normalization ($b$):} It penalizes longer documents that might contain a term many times simply due to their length, ensuring that concise, relevant postings are not overshadowed.
\end{itemize}
BM25 remains a powerful baseline, especially for domain-specific technical terms (e.g., "Kubernetes", "TypeScript") where exact matching is critical.

\section{Vector Representations}
Vector-based retrieval (dense retrieval) represents text as high-dimensional numerical vectors, where the geometric distance between vectors correlates with the semantic similarity of the underlying text.

\subsection{Dense Embeddings}
Unlike sparse vectors (where each dimension corresponds to a unique word), dense embeddings are typically 384 to 1536 dimensions long. These vectors are generated by pre-trained neural networks, most notably those based on the Transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention}.

\subsection{Sentence-BERT (SBERT) and Bi-Encoders}
Standard BERT models are not naturally suited for large-scale similarity searches because they require "cross-encoding" (passing a query-document pair through the model together), which is computationally prohibitive. Sentence-BERT \cite{reimers2019sentence} introduced the \textit{bi-encoder} architecture. It processes the query and the document independently to produce fixed-sized embeddings. Similarity is then calculated using a simple metric like Cosine Similarity:
\begin{equation}
    \text{similarity} = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{A}\| \|\mathbf{B}\|}
\end{equation}
This allows for the pre-computation and indexing of document vectors, making real-time semantic search feasible.

\section{Hybrid Retrieval}
In the context of internship listings, neither keyword nor vector search is perfect in isolation. Keyword search fails to find "Software Developer" if the user searches for "Programmer". Conversely, vector search might struggle with highly specific version numbers or rare library names if they were not well-represented in the training data.

Hybrid retrieval combines the strengths of both:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sparse Retrieval (BM25)} handles exact matches, acronyms, and rare technical terms.
    \item \textbf{Dense Retrieval (SBERT)} handles synonyms, paraphrasing, and conceptual queries.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)}
A common method to combine these disparate scores is Reciprocal Rank Fusion \cite{corback2009rrf}. It calculates a final score based on the rank of a document in each retrieval system, rather than the raw score values:
\begin{equation}
    RRFscore(d \in D) = \frac{1}{k + r(d)}
\end{equation}
where $R$ is the set of rankers, $r(d)$ is the rank of document $d$, and $k$ is a constant (typically 60) to mitigate the impact of low-ranked outliers.

\section{Efficiency Considerations}
Scaling semantic search to thousands or millions of job postings requires specialized infrastructure to manage high-dimensional vector operations.

\subsection{Vector Databases and ANN}
Traditional SQL databases are not optimized for nearest neighbor searches in high-dimensional space. Vector databases (e.g., Milvus, Pinecone, or the HNSW index in OpenSearch) utilize \textit{Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN)} algorithms to trade a small amount of accuracy for significant speedups.

\subsection{HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World)}
HNSW is one of the most popular ANN algorithms. It constructs a multi-layered graph where the top layers contain long-range edges for fast navigation and the bottom layers contain short-range edges for local precision. This structure allows for logarithmic search complexity, making it suitable for low-latency production environments.

\subsection{Quantization}
To reduce memory footprint, techniques like \textit{Scalar Quantization} or \textit{Product Quantization (PQ)} are used. These compress floating-point vectors into lower-precision representations, significantly reducing the RAM required to store the index while maintaining acceptable retrieval recall.

\chapter{Related Work}
The automation of recruitment and labor market analysis has seen significant progress with the advent of Natural Language Processing (NLP) and machine learning. This chapter reviews the existing literature and technological approaches relevant to job posting analytics, semantic alignment, and the trade-offs between modern large-scale models and specialized lightweight pipelines.

\section{Job Posting Analytics and Skill Extraction}
The extraction of structured information from job advertisements is a well-established research area, often referred to as Job Posting Analytics (JPA). Early approaches relied heavily on rule-based systems and gazetteers to identify key entities like job titles and locations \cite{tamburri2020data}. However, the high variability of natural language in job descriptions led to the adoption of statistical and deep learning methods.

Recent research has focused on fine-tuning Transformer-based models, such as BERT, specifically for the recruitment domain. For instance, Bhola et al. \cite{bhola2020extracting} demonstrated that domain-specific pre-training significantly improves the accuracy of skill extraction compared to general-purpose NER models. Similarly, Sayfullina et al. \cite{sayfullina2018learning} explored the use of skill embeddings to capture the latent relationships between technologies, allowing systems to understand that "React" and "Vue" are semantically related even if they appear in different documents. Gnehm and Clematide \cite{gnehm2022extraction} further extended this by analyzing how the linguistic context of a skill (e.g., "required" vs. "beneficial") can be automatically classified to provide deeper insights into employer demands.

\section{Ontology/Taxonomy Alignment in HR/Recruitment}
A core challenge in HR tech is the "vocabulary gap" between different actors in the labor market. Standardized taxonomies like ESCO \cite{esco_portal} and O*NET \cite{onet_online} provide a common reference point, but mapping raw, non-standardized text to these hierarchies is a non-trivial task known as Entity Linking or Taxonomy Alignment.

Literature in this area often discusses the trade-off between the expressiveness of full ontologies and the practical utility of flatter taxonomies. While ontologies can model complex relationships (e.g., "Python is a prerequisite for Data Science"), they often suffer from "ontology drift" as new technologies emerge. Lightweight semantic approaches focus on alignment at the embedding level, where raw strings are mapped to the nearest canonical concept in a vector space. This allows for a more flexible and maintainable system that can handle the rapid evolution of technical skills without requiring constant manual updates to a formal logic-based ontology.

\section{LLM-based Pipelines vs Lightweight Pipelines}
The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 has introduced a new paradigm for Information Extraction. These models exhibit remarkable zero-shot and few-shot capabilities, often outperforming specialized fine-tuned models on complex extraction tasks without requiring extensive labeled datasets \cite{wei2023chatgpt}.

However, recent comparative studies have highlighted several drawbacks of relying solely on LLMs for production-scale job posting pipelines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latency and Throughput:} Processing thousands of scraped job posts per hour using cloud-based LLM APIs can be prohibitively slow and expensive.
    \item \textbf{Consistency and Hallucination:} LLMs may occasionally "hallucinate" skills that are not present in the text or format the output inconsistently, complicating downstream database integration.
    \item \textbf{Privacy and Data Sovereignty:} Sending sensitive recruitment data to external APIs may conflict with regional data protection regulations (e.g., GDPR).
\end{itemize}
In contrast, lightweight pipelines—combining optimized small-scale models (like DistilBERT) with heuristic rules and local vector databases—offer a more cost-effective and predictable alternative. This thesis argues for a hybrid approach where LLMs are used for high-quality data annotation and initial schema generation, while the runtime production system utilizes a lightweight, modular architecture for efficiency and control.

\chapter{Methodology and System Overview}

\section{Design Goals and Constraints}
To address the challenges identified in the recruitment domain, the system is developed with the following primary objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low Computational Cost per Document:} The system must be capable of running on commodity hardware without requiring expensive GPU clusters for real-time inference. This is achieved by utilizing small-scale transformer models (e.g., DistilBERT) and efficient regex-based pre-filters.
    \item \textbf{Robustness to Noisy Text and HTML Variability:} Since inputs originate from diverse scrapers and user-provided CSVs, the pipeline must handle inconsistent formatting, boilerplate text, and encoding errors gracefully.
    \item \textbf{Explainability (Traceable Extraction Decisions):} Unlike "black-box" LLM prompts, the system should provide clear evidence for its extractions. Every structured field should be traceable back to specific spans in the source text or specific rules in the knowledge base.
    \item \textbf{Extensibility:} It must be possible to add support for new skills, job categories, or input formats without necessitating a complete retraining of the core models.
\end{itemize}

The system follows a modular pipeline architecture designed for high throughput and maintainability.

\section{Data Flow}
The data flow within the system follows a linear progression with intermediate validation checkpoints:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Acquisition:} Raw HTML or CSV rows enter the system. A source-specific adapter normalizes the input into a standard "RawDocument" schema.
    \item \textbf{Sanitization:} Heuristic rules strip known boilerplate (e.g., footer links). Language detection ensures only supported languages proceed to the NLP stages.
    \item \textbf{Extraction:} Parallel modules extract entities. A high-recall regex module identifies common technical terms, while a transformer-based bi-encoder maps complex descriptions to the closest taxonomy labels.
    \item \textbf{Reconciliation:} Conflicting extractions are resolved using a confidence-based scoring system.
    \item \textbf{Persistence:} The final "StructuredDocument" is committed to the relational database and the vector index simultaneously.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Web Scraping Strategy}
To build a representative dataset of internship opportunities, we implemented a distributed scraping strategy targeting major job aggregation platforms and specific company career portals. The strategy is designed to maximize data diversity while adhering to ethical scraping practices.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Targeted Sources:} We prioritized platforms with high-density internship listings, such as LinkedIn, Indeed, and specialized student career portals (e.g., Handshake, Welcome to the Jungle).
    \item \textbf{Pagination and Depth:} Scrapers were configured to traverse search results up to a depth of 50 pages per query, ensuring a mix of recent and slightly older listings for temporal analysis.
    \item \textbf{Rate Limiting and Politeness:} To avoid overwhelming source servers, we implemented adaptive rate limiting and random delays between requests. User-agent rotation was used to simulate diverse client environments.
    \item \textbf{Incremental Scraping:} To maintain an up-to-date repository, the system performs incremental updates, identifying and skipping previously processed URLs based on their cryptographic hashes.
\end{itemize}

\section{HTML Parsing and Text Extraction}
Web-scraped data is inherently "noisy," containing navigation menus, advertisements, and tracking scripts that are irrelevant to the job description.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Boilerplate Removal:} We utilize library-based parsers (e.g., BeautifulSoup4 or Selectolax) to strip non-content tags such as \texttt{<script>}, \texttt{<style>}, \texttt{<nav>}, and \texttt{<footer>}.
    \item \textbf{Content Extraction Heuristics:} We employ a density-based approach to identify the main "Job Description" block. Containers with a high ratio of text to HTML tags are prioritized. Common CSS classes like \texttt{.job-description} or \texttt{\#vacancy-content} are used as initial anchors.
    \item \textbf{Structural Preservation:} While stripping HTML, it is crucial to preserve certain structural elements that carry semantic meaning. We convert list items (\texttt{<li>}) to bullet points and headers (\texttt{<h1>} to \texttt{<h3>}) to markdown-style markers to help the NLP models understand the grouping of requirements and benefits.
\end{itemize}

\section{CSV Upload Handling}
In addition to automated scraping, the system supports manual ingestion of internship data via CSV files. This allows recruitment agencies and university career centers to integrate their internal datasets into the pipeline.

\subsection{Column Mapping Heuristics}
User-provided CSVs rarely follow a fixed schema. To provide a seamless user experience, we implemented a heuristic-based column mapper:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fuzzy Header Matching:} The system compares CSV headers against a set of canonical field names (e.g., "Job Title", "Description", "Location", "Company") using Levenshtein distance.
    \item \textbf{Content-Based Inference:} If headers are missing or ambiguous (e.g., "Col\_1"), the system analyzes the first 10 rows. Columns with long text blocks are flagged as "Description" candidates, while those with short, repetitive strings are flagged as "Category" or "Location".
    \item \textbf{User Override:} The inferred mapping is presented to the user for validation and manual correction before final processing.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Validation and Sanitization}
To ensure system stability, all uploaded data undergoes rigorous validation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Encoding Detection:} We use the \texttt{chardet} library to identify and normalize various character encodings (e.g., UTF-8, Latin-1) to a consistent UTF-8 format.
    \item \textbf{Data Sanitization:} String fields are stripped of leading/trailing whitespace and control characters. HTML tags accidentally included in CSV cells are escaped or removed.
    \item \textbf{Integrity Checks:} Rows missing critical fields (e.g., a missing job title or description) are rejected or flagged for manual review to prevent downstream errors.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Semantic Schema and Label Space}
This chapter defines the internal data model and the controlled vocabularies used to transform raw internship listings into a structured format. We describe the transition from a "RawDocument" to a "StructuredDocument" and the mechanisms used to bridge the gap between diverse natural language expressions and standardized taxonomies.

\section{Target Schema}
The target schema serves as the blueprint for the extraction process. It defines the specific fields that the enrichment pipeline (detailed in Chapter 8) aims to populate. Unlike the "RawDocument" schema, which preserves all original artifacts, the "StructuredDocument" schema is optimized for retrieval and analytical consistency.

\subsection{Field Definitions}
The core fields of the schema are categorized into three groups: identifying metadata, descriptive attributes, and semantic labels.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metadata:} Includes a unique \texttt{uuid}, the \texttt{source\_url} or \texttt{file\_origin}, and the \texttt{ingestion\_timestamp}.
    \item \textbf{Textual Attributes:} The \texttt{title\_raw} and \texttt{description\_clean} are preserved to allow for highlighting in search results and for manual verification of extracted data.
    \item \textbf{Semantic Fields:} These are the primary targets for normalization:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{canonical\_job\_role}: A reference to a specific node in the occupation taxonomy.
        \item \texttt{skills\_list}: A collection of unique identifiers representing extracted competencies.
        \item \texttt{location\_structured}: A JSON object containing normalized city, region, and country codes.
        \item \texttt{experience\_level}: A categorical value (e.g., Junior, Intern, Lead).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Design Rationale}
The schema adopts a \textit{denormalized approach} for the "skills" and "tags" fields. While a relational database typically suggests a many-to-many junction table, our search-centric design stores these as arrays of identifiers within the document. This significantly reduces join overhead during complex filtering operations in the search index.

\section{Canonical Labels}
To prevent data fragmentation, the system maps raw strings to a fixed set of canonical labels. As discussed in Chapter 2, we rely on the \textbf{ESCO (European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations)} framework as our primary label space.

\subsection{Occupation Taxonomy}
Raw job titles vary significantly across companies (e.g., "Happiness Engineer" vs. "Customer Support"). We map these to the ESCO Occupation pillar, typically at the fourth level of hierarchy. This level provides sufficient granularity (e.g., "Web Developer" vs. "Mobile App Developer") without becoming overly specific to the point of sparsity.

\subsection{Skill Taxonomy}
The ESCO skills pillar contains over 13,000 concepts. We filter this space to focus on "Information and Communication Technologies" and "Business and Administration" to keep the label space manageable for our lightweight classifiers. Each canonical skill is associated with a URI, a preferred label, and a set of hidden labels.

\section{Synonyms and Alias Tables}
The "semantic gap" is bridged through a robust mapping layer consisting of manually curated and automatically expanded alias tables.

\subsection{Lexical Normalization}
Before semantic mapping, raw tokens undergo lexical normalization. This includes case folding, punctuation removal, and the resolution of common abbreviations. For example, "Sr." is normalized to "Senior", and "Int." is expanded to "Internship" or "International" depending on the field context.

\subsection{The Alias Lookup Mechanism}
The system maintains a lookup table where multiple "surface forms" map to a single "canonical ID". This is particularly critical for technologies and tools:

\subsection{Dynamic Alias Generation}
While the core alias tables are static, we supplement them with a dynamic expansion module. This module uses the Word2Vec or FastText embeddings discussed in Chapter 3 to identify terms that are semantically identical but missing from the lookup table. If a term like "Golang" frequently appears in the same context as "Go (programming language)", the system flags it as a potential alias for review.
