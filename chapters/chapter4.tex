\chapter{Related Work}
The automation of recruitment and labor market analysis has seen significant progress with the advent of Natural Language Processing (NLP) and machine learning \cite{tamburri2020data, faliagka2012online, lacic2020recommender}. This chapter reviews the existing literature and technological approaches relevant to job posting analytics, semantic alignment, and the trade-offs between modern large-scale models and specialized lightweight pipelines.

\section{Job Posting Analytics and Skill Extraction}
The extraction of structured information from job advertisements is a well-established research area, often referred to as Job Posting Analytics (JPA) \cite{tamburri2020data, boselli2018classifying, kurekova2015using}. Early approaches relied heavily on rule-based systems and gazetteers to identify key entities like job titles and locations \cite{sarawagi2008information}. However, the high variability of natural language in job descriptions led to the adoption of statistical and deep learning methods \cite{li2020survey, wadden2019entity}.

Recent research has focused on fine-tuning Transformer-based models, such as BERT, specifically for the recruitment domain \cite{bhola2020retrieving, pappas2022jobbert}. Domain-specific pre-training has been shown to significantly improve the accuracy of skill extraction compared to general-purpose NER models. Other studies have explored the use of skill embeddings and representation learning to capture the latent relationships between technologies \cite{zhao2018job2vec, giabelli2021skills}, allowing systems to understand that "React" and "Vue" are semantically related even if they appear in different documents \cite{giabelli2021skills}. Furthermore, analyzing how the linguistic context of a skill (e.g., "required" vs. "beneficial") can be automatically classified provides deeper insights into employer demands \cite{qin2018enhancing}.

\section{Ontology/Taxonomy Alignment in HR/Recruitment}
A core challenge in HR tech is the "vocabulary gap" between different actors in the labor market. Standardized taxonomies like ESCO and O*NET provide a common reference point \cite{peterson2001occupational}, but mapping raw, non-standardized text to these hierarchies is a non-trivial task known as Entity Linking or Taxonomy Alignment \cite{zhang2022survey}. Modern approaches often leverage Recruitment Knowledge Graphs to maintain these complex relationships \cite{shi2020recruitment}.

Literature in this area often discusses the trade-off between the expressiveness of full ontologies and the practical utility of flatter taxonomies \cite{staab2009handbook}. While ontologies can model complex relationships (e.g., "Python is a prerequisite for Data Science"), they often suffer from "ontology drift" as new technologies emerge, requiring sophisticated matching and evolution strategies \cite{shvaiko2013ontology}. Lightweight semantic approaches focus on alignment at the embedding level, where raw strings are mapped to the nearest canonical concept in a vector space \cite{javed2021review, giabelli2021skills}. Specific challenges in normalizing idiosyncratic job titles into canonical forms have also been explored using compound title learning \cite{sayfullina2018learning}.

\section{LLM-based Pipelines vs Lightweight Pipelines}
The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) has introduced a new paradigm for Information Extraction, moving from "pre-train and fine-tune" to "pre-train, prompt, and predict" \cite{brown2020language, liu2023pre}. These models exhibit remarkable zero-shot and few-shot capabilities, particularly when using advanced techniques like Chain-of-Thought prompting \cite{wei2022chain}, often outperforming specialized fine-tuned models on complex extraction tasks without requiring extensive labeled datasets.

However, recent comparative studies have highlighted several drawbacks of relying solely on LLMs for production-scale job posting pipelines \cite{hsieh2023distilling, ji2023survey, bender2021dangers}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latency and Throughput:} Processing thousands of scraped job posts per hour using cloud-based LLM APIs can be prohibitively slow and expensive.
    \item \textbf{Consistency and Hallucination:} LLMs may occasionally "hallucinate" skills that are not present in the text or format the output inconsistently, a known limitation in current generative architectures \cite{ji2023survey, bender2021dangers}.
    \item \textbf{Privacy and Data Sovereignty:} Sending sensitive recruitment data to external APIs may conflict with regional data protection regulations (e.g., GDPR), raising significant ethical and legal concerns \cite{hunkenschroer2022ethics}.
\end{itemize}
In contrast, lightweight pipelines—combining optimized small-scale models like DistilBERT with heuristic rules and local vector databases—offer a more cost-effective and predictable alternative. This is often achieved through Knowledge Distillation, where a smaller "student" model learns to replicate the performance of a larger "teacher" model \cite{hsieh2023distilling, menghani2023efficient}. This thesis argues for a hybrid approach where LLMs are used for high-quality data annotation and initial schema generation, while the runtime production system utilizes a lightweight, modular architecture for efficiency and control, adhering to the principles of "Green AI" \cite{schwartz2020green} and efficient deep learning practices \cite{menghani2023efficient}.
