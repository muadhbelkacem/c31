\chapter{Background: Information Extraction and Semantics}

This chapter lays the theoretical groundwork for the \texttt{internships-code} system.
Our goal here is to connect the dots between classical Information Extraction (IE) theory~\cite{sarawagi2008information} and the real-world demands of a modern recruitment data pipeline.
We will look at how messy, semi-structured internship postings get turned into something clean, structured, and genuinely searchable.
Along the way, we will draw on established ideas from data matching~\cite{christen2012data}, natural language processing~\cite{jurafsky2023speech}, and occupational taxonomies, while also keeping an eye on the emerging role of Large Language Models (LLMs)~\cite{brown2020language}.
This backdrop is essential for understanding the system's central architectural bet: a lightweight, cost-conscious pipeline that saves heavy-duty AI for only the trickiest edge cases.

\section{The Data Landscape: Making Sense of Messy Information}

At the heart of recruitment informatics lies a stubborn problem: data sources are wildly different from one another.
Internship listings do not come in one neat format.
Instead, they are scattered across a spectrum---from completely free-form text to rigidly structured databases.
Navigating this landscape is, at its core, a data integration challenge~\cite{doan2012principles}.

\subsection{Unstructured vs.\ Semi-Structured Data}

Understanding the difference between unstructured and semi-structured data is crucial when designing any processing pipeline.

\textbf{Unstructured Data} is the hard stuff.
Think of a raw cover letter or the free-text body of a job description.
There is no predefined model telling you where the key information lives.
A human can read it and understand, but for a machine, all the meaning is buried in the prose.

\textbf{Semi-Structured Data} is what you typically get from web scraping.
It has organizational markers---HTML tags, CSS classes, JSON keys---that provide a loose sense of structure.
But do not be fooled: it is not a proper database.
You might find a salary tucked inside a \texttt{<span class="salary">} element, but the actual value will still be a messy string like ``Competitive'' or ``\$20-\$25/hr''~\cite{abiteboul1997querying}.

Our system's ingestion module wrestles directly with what you might call a ``schema-on-read'' problem.
Different platforms use different keys for the exact same thing: \texttt{platform\_link}, \texttt{url}, \texttt{apply\_href}---all meaning the same destination.
A core job of the preprocessing layer is \textit{schema mapping}---smoothing out these inconsistencies by normalizing everything into a single, unified field like \texttt{link}.

\subsection{Noise and the Art of Cleaning}

Web-scraped text is inherently noisy.
It is full of boilerplate (legal disclaimers, navigation menus, footer links) and formatting artifacts (rivers of whitespace, HTML entities, broken character encodings).
Let us be clear: data cleaning is not just housekeeping.
It is an absolute prerequisite.
The quality of everything that comes later---every extraction, every classification---is bounded by how clean the input is~\cite{chu2016data}.

Our preprocessing pipeline handles this with a few key operations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Text Normalization} transforms text into a consistent, canonical form.
    The \texttt{cleanDescription} utility handles the basics: lowercasing everything, collapsing whitespace, stripping leftover HTML.
    This reduction in surface-level variation dramatically lightens the load on whatever NLP models come next, whether they are simple lexicons or transformer-based classifiers.

    \item \textbf{Tokenization} is the critical first step for most IE and Information Retrieval (IR) tasks---breaking continuous text into discrete tokens (words, punctuation, subwords)~\cite{manning2008introduction}.
    Consistency here is non-negotiable, especially for the dictionary-based matching we use in the first pass of skill extraction.
    If the tokenization strategy does not match between the lexicon and the input text, things fail silently.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Fundamentals of Information Extraction}

Information Extraction is the process of automatically pulling structured, machine-readable information---think rows in a database---out of unstructured or semi-structured documents~\cite{grishman2015information, sarawagi2008information}.
In our context, IE is what turns a narrative job posting into a formal \texttt{Internship} object with actual fields: title, skills, salary.

\subsection{Named Entity Recognition (NER)}

NER is a foundational IE subtask.
It finds and classifies named entities in text into predefined buckets like ``Person,'' ``Organization,'' ``Location''---or, in our world, ``Skill,'' ``Job Title,'' and ``Salary''~\cite{nadeau2007survey}.
Our system uses NER at multiple levels of sophistication:

\textbf{Dictionary-Based (Gazetteer) NER} relies on a carefully curated list of known entities---a gazetteer.
The \texttt{extractSkills} function works this way, scanning text for skill terms we already know about.
Yes, this approach is limited: it cannot spot a brand-new technology or a skill expressed in an unexpected way.
But what it offers in return is valuable---extremely high precision, perfect recall on its known terms, and exceptional speed~\cite{nadeau2007survey}.
As the first layer in a multi-tiered pipeline, it handles the bulk of straightforward cases at zero inference cost.

\textbf{Neural NER} takes a more sophisticated approach.
Modern systems use sequence labeling models like BiLSTM-CRF or transformer architectures like BERT to classify entities based on their surrounding context~\cite{lample2016neural, li2020survey}.
This matters because context resolves ambiguity---and ambiguity is everywhere.
The word ``Java'' could mean a programming language, an Indonesian island, or coffee.
Only context can tell you which one matters.
In our pipeline, a lightweight DistilBERT model serves as the second tier, stepping in for trickier cases and extending the system's reach beyond what the lexicon alone can capture.

\subsection{Slot Filling and Attribute Extraction}

Slot filling---or relation extraction at the entity level---is about identifying specific values for predefined attributes (or ``slots'') tied to an entity.
Here, the entity is a job posting, and the slots are its attributes: salary, location, required degree, and so on.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regex-Based Extraction:} For highly predictable text patterns---salary strings like ``\$70k--\$90k,'' dates, email addresses---Regular Expressions (Regex) are a precise, efficient, and easy-to-maintain tool.
    The \texttt{cleanSalary} module uses a tiered set of regex patterns to parse, normalize, and convert the bewildering variety of salary expressions into a clean numeric range~\cite{sarawagi2008information}.

    \item \textbf{Heuristic Validation:} After extraction, values pass through a sanity check.
    A parsed salary has to fall within a believable range.
    A date has to form a valid calendar date.
    This contextual post-processing provides a baseline level of data integrity before anything touches the database.
\end{itemize}

\section{Bridging the Semantic Gap with Taxonomies}

One of the central puzzles this thesis tackles is the ``semantic gap''---the distance between the specific, often quirky language an employer uses in a job posting and the formal, general categories a job seeker would actually search for.
A position called ``Code Wizard'' will never surface for a student searching ``Software Engineer'' unless we actively bridge that gap.

\subsection{Occupational Taxonomies}

The most robust solution is to map raw, non-standard text onto a canonical, standardized occupational taxonomy.
This gives the entire labor market a shared reference language.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ESCO (European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations):} This multilingual classification system---our primary reference in this research---organizes occupations into a hierarchy and explicitly links them to required skills and competences.
    Its dual structure (occupations and skills) makes it a natural fit as a target schema for semantic enrichment.

    \item \textbf{O*NET (Occupational Information Network):} A similar framework used mainly in the United States.
    The core principles---hierarchical mapping, linking roles to skills---are consistent across both systems, and our design is fundamentally taxonomy-agnostic~\cite{peterson2001occupational}.

    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Mapping:} By anchoring a job posting to a taxonomy node, the system inherits a whole network of professional relationships.
    A ``React Developer'' is automatically understood as a kind of ``Front-End Developer'' and, more broadly, a ``Software Engineer.''
    This enables powerful faceted search and discovery that pure keyword systems simply cannot replicate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Heuristic vs.\ Semantic Classification}

Our approach to classification follows the same tiered philosophy as our NER strategy.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Classification (Layer A):} For categorical filtering, we start with a high-precision keyword-anchoring approach.
    A title containing ``Accountant'' maps directly to ``Finance.''
    This deterministic, zero-cost layer handles the clear-cut majority with perfect accuracy and speed---exactly what you need in a production system facing high data volumes~\cite{boselli2018classifying}.

    \item \textbf{Semantic Classification (Layer B):} Some titles are too vague (``Consultant'') or too idiosyncratic (``Growth Hacker'') for simple keyword matching.
    Here, we turn to \textit{sentence embeddings} from a lightweight bi-encoder model like Sentence-BERT (SBERT)~\cite{reimers2019sentence}.
    The title's embedding is compared against pre-computed embeddings of all ESCO occupation labels, and we assign the nearest neighbor.
    This dramatically boosts recall for non-standard roles~\cite{sayfullina2018learning}.
    We chose a bi-encoder architecture deliberately: document embeddings can be pre-computed, and cosine similarity search can be accelerated with vector databases---keeping things fast and efficient.
\end{itemize}

\section{Record Linkage and Finding Duplicates}

When you aggregate data from multiple job boards, you create a new problem: duplicates.
The same internship often appears on LinkedIn, Indeed, and a university portal, each with slight variations in title, description, or formatting.

\subsection{Deduplication Strategies}

\textit{Record Linkage} (or Entity Resolution) is the task of algorithmically identifying which records across datasets refer to the same real-world entity~\cite{christen2012data, elmagarmid2007duplicate}.
Our system weaves this logic directly into the ingestion pipeline.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Matching via Blocking:} The \texttt{generateId} function creates a canonical identifier by combining a ``slugified'' company name with a normalized job title.
    This ID serves as a \textit{blocking key}.
    By only comparing records that share this exact key, we limit the expensive work of pairwise comparison to small, manageable blocks---dramatically speeding things up~\cite{christen2012data}.
    This is our primary deduplication strategy.

    \item \textbf{Probabilistic Matching:} Deterministic blocking falls short when titles are slightly rephrased---``Software Eng.\ Intern'' vs.\ ``Intern, Software Engineering.''
    For these cases, a second pass can apply fuzzy string metrics like Levenshtein distance or Jaccard similarity on the blocked groups to calculate similarity scores and resolve near-duplicates~\cite{elmagarmid2007duplicate}.
    For now, we rely mainly on deterministic blocking, keeping probabilistic resolution as an optional offline batch process.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Canonicalization and the Golden Record}

Once we have linked a group of duplicate records, we need a single ``golden'' representation for storage and indexing.
The heuristic for this might prefer the most authoritative source, the most recently updated posting, or the one with the most complete information.
The goal is a search index that is both concise and accurate---no redundant clutter~\cite{doan2012principles}.

\section{The Shifting Paradigm: Rules, Models, and LLMs}

It is worth placing our architecture in the broader IE landscape.
The field has evolved from rule-based systems (1990s--2000s) to statistical and neural models (2010s), and now to the era of Large Language Models~\cite{brown2020language}.
LLMs have shown remarkable zero-shot extraction abilities, often outperforming specialist models without any domain-specific training.
With techniques like Chain-of-Thought prompting, a single LLM call could theoretically handle everything we have discussed in this chapter---NER, slot-filling, taxonomy mapping---all at once~\cite{wei2022chain}.

But raw power comes with real operational costs.
For a production system processing thousands of new postings daily, an LLM-only architecture brings serious drawbacks: latency, cost, and inconsistency~\cite{bender2021dangers, ji2023survey}.

The \texttt{internships-code} system embraces a different philosophy: the \textbf{tiered pipeline}.
We build on a foundation of free, fast, and fully explainable deterministic rules (Layer~A) that handle the straightforward majority.
A lightweight, fine-tuned neural model (Layer~B) takes on the next tier of semantic complexity.
Only the genuinely ambiguous edge cases---if any---are escalated to a powerful but expensive LLM (Layer~C).
This architecture is a direct expression of ``Green AI'' principles: prioritizing computational efficiency and sustainability without giving up the scalability or practical accuracy the problem demands~\cite{schwartz2020green, menghani2023efficient}.
