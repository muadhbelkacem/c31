\chapter{Background, Related Work, and Theoretical Foundations}

\label{chap:background}

\section{Chapter Introduction}

This chapter establishes the conceptual and scholarly foundation of the thesis. It situates internship discovery within modern labor-market information systems and reviews literature relevant to semantic processing of recruitment data. The chapter presents the theoretical concepts that motivate the proposed research problem, including information extraction, occupational classification frameworks, lexical matching approaches, semantic classification methods, and lightweight embedding-based semantic processing.

The objective of this chapter is not to describe the implementation of the proposed system, but rather to explain the scientific background and prior research that inform its architectural and methodological design choices.

\section{Internship Search and Labor-Market Information Systems}

Internship discovery systems operate at the intersection of labor economics, information retrieval, natural language processing, and software engineering. Modern recruitment ecosystems rely heavily on online platforms where employers publish opportunities using heterogeneous terminology, formatting conventions, and domain-specific vocabulary \cite{manning2008introduction}.

Unlike traditional databases built around fixed schemas, internship listings are frequently noisy, semi-structured, and distributed across multiple sources. Equivalent roles may be described using different job titles, skill expressions, or organizational language. Consequently, exact lexical search alone often fails to capture semantic similarity between employer descriptions and user intent.

Recruitment platforms therefore require mechanisms for semantic normalization, structured representation, and scalable retrieval. These requirements motivate the use of information extraction techniques, occupational taxonomies, and hybrid retrieval architectures.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Job Posting Analytics and Skill Extraction}

Job Posting Analytics (JPA) focuses on extracting structured information from recruitment advertisements and labor-market data. Common objectives include identifying skills, occupations, educational requirements, salaries, and experience levels from textual descriptions \cite{senger2024deep}.

Early work in this area relied heavily on rule-based methods and manually curated dictionaries. Such approaches provided interpretability and precision but struggled with linguistic variation and domain drift. Later developments introduced statistical learning and neural architectures capable of capturing contextual meaning \cite{devlin2019bert}.

Transformer-based models have significantly influenced recruitment analytics by enabling context-aware extraction and classification. Domain adaptation studies demonstrate improvements in handling ambiguous titles, non-standard skill expressions, and industry-specific terminology. However, the computational cost of large neural systems remains an important operational concern.

\subsection{Taxonomy Alignment in Recruitment Systems}

A central challenge in labor-market analytics is the vocabulary gap between employer language and standardized occupational representations. Research and industrial recruitment systems often employ occupational classification frameworks to support normalization and semantic comparison.

ESCO and O*NET are among the most widely used occupational knowledge frameworks \cite{esco2024, peterson2001occupational}. These resources organize occupations, skills, competences, and qualifications into structured reference spaces that support matching, categorization, and analysis.

Research on taxonomy alignment investigates how informal textual mentions can be mapped to structured occupational categories. Existing approaches include rule-based normalization, embedding-based semantic matching, ontology alignment techniques, and knowledge-graph methods \cite{staab2009handbook, shvaiko2013ontology}.

While highly expressive ontological systems can capture rich semantic relations, they often introduce maintenance complexity and sensitivity to evolving labor-market terminology. Lightweight semantic representations therefore remain attractive for systems that prioritize operational feasibility.

\subsection{LLM-Based Versus Lightweight Semantic Pipelines}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed contemporary NLP by demonstrating strong zero-shot and few-shot capabilities across information extraction tasks \cite{brown2020language, devlin2019bert}. Their flexibility enables semantic interpretation without extensive task-specific engineering.

Nevertheless, production-scale deployment raises important challenges. Processing large recruitment corpora through cloud-based LLM services may introduce latency, cost, reproducibility, and privacy concerns. These constraints are particularly relevant for systems processing large volumes of externally collected recruitment listings \cite{bender2021dangers}.

Consequently, recent literature increasingly discusses trade-offs between heavyweight generative architectures and lightweight modular semantic pipelines. Smaller transformer models and hybrid processing strategies seek to balance semantic capability with computational efficiency \cite{menghani2023efficient, schwartz2020green}.

\section{Information Extraction and Semantic Structuring}

\subsection{Heterogeneous Recruitment Data}

Recruitment data exists across a spectrum ranging from unstructured prose to partially structured web content. Job descriptions, application requirements, and employer narratives often appear as free text, whereas scraped HTML pages and uploaded JSON files provide partial structural cues \cite{abiteboul1997querying, doan2012principles}.

This diversity creates a data integration challenge. Different providers may use distinct naming conventions, schema layouts, and formatting styles to represent equivalent concepts. Effective processing therefore requires normalization, cleaning, and schema harmonization \cite{chu2016data}.

\subsection{Information Extraction Fundamentals}

Information Extraction (IE) concerns the transformation of unstructured or semi-structured documents into structured representations \cite{jurafsky2023speech}. In recruitment contexts, IE enables the conversion of narrative descriptions into machine-readable attributes.

Important IE subtasks include \cite{sarawagi2008information, grishman2015information}:

\begin{itemize}
\item Named Entity Recognition (NER)
\item Attribute extraction
\item Relation extraction
\item Slot filling
\item Classification and normalization
\end{itemize}

NER identifies relevant entities such as organizations, locations, occupations, salaries, or skills. Attribute extraction associates these entities with structured properties. Together, these mechanisms support searchable and analyzable recruitment records.

\subsection{Occupational Taxonomies and Semantic Normalization}

Semantic normalization addresses variation in recruitment language by linking heterogeneous expressions to shared conceptual representations \cite{staab2009handbook}.

Occupational taxonomies provide one mechanism for achieving this objective. By mapping non-standard titles and skills into canonical categories, systems can improve comparability across employers, industries, and platforms.

Hierarchical taxonomies additionally support semantic generalization. Closely related occupations can be interpreted within broader professional families, improving discovery beyond exact lexical matching.

\subsection{Record Linkage and Deduplication}

Aggregating listings from multiple platforms introduces the problem of duplicate or near-duplicate
records. Equivalent internships may appear under slightly different titles, descriptions, or formatting conventions.
Record linkage research addresses this challenge by identifying records that refer to the same
real-world entity \cite{christen2012data, elmagarmid2007duplicate}. Approaches include deterministic blocking, similarity scoring, probabilistic
matching, and clustering methods.
Effective deduplication improves index quality, reduces redundancy, and supports more reliable retrieval performance.

\section{Text Representation and Retrieval}

\subsection{Lexical Retrieval and Keyword Search}

Traditional information retrieval systems primarily rely on lexical matching between query terms and document content \cite{manning2008introduction, robertson2009probabilistic, zobel2006inverted}. Sparse retrieval methods remain widely used because of their efficiency, transparency, and mature engineering foundations.

Keyword-based matching represents a foundational approach to information discovery, relying on term-frequency formulations and document-level relevance estimation.

Despite its strengths, lexical matching exhibits limitations when equivalent meanings are expressed using different vocabulary.

\subsection{Dense Representations and Sentence Embeddings}

Dense retrieval methods address lexical mismatch by representing text in continuous vector spaces. Neural encoders transform documents and queries into embeddings whose geometric proximity approximates semantic similarity \cite{karpukhin2020dense, luan2021sparse}.

Transformer-derived sentence representations have become especially important for semantic search. Sentence-level embedding methods allow efficient similarity computation and support scalable retrieval workflows \cite{reimers2019sentence}.

Dense representations improve handling of paraphrases, conceptual similarity, and non-standard terminology.

\subsection{Hybrid Retrieval}

Because lexical and dense retrieval possess complementary strengths, modern systems increasingly employ hybrid retrieval strategies \cite{bruch2023analysis}.

Lexical search remains strong for exact terminology, acronyms, and rare technical terms, whereas semantic retrieval captures conceptual similarity and vocabulary variation. Combining both modalities often improves robustness across diverse query types.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion and related score-combination techniques are common mechanisms for integrating sparse and dense rankings \cite{cormack2009reciprocal, bruch2023analysis}.

\section{The Gap Between Current Problems and Existing Solutions}

\subsection{Limitations of General Recruitment Platforms}

Existing research and recruitment platforms provide useful foundations for job posting analytics, information extraction, occupational taxonomy alignment, semantic processing, and recruitment data organization. However, these solutions do not fully address the specific requirements of internship-focused discovery. General recruitment platforms are usually designed for broad job search rather than dedicated internship filtering. As a result, internship opportunities are often mixed with full-time jobs, graduate programs, training advertisements, senior roles, and unrelated postings.

\subsection{Incomplete Use of Information Extraction}

Information extraction methods can help transform recruitment text into structured data by identifying useful attributes such as job titles, companies, locations, salaries, skills, and requirements. However, these methods are often applied to isolated extraction tasks rather than to a complete internship-focused processing workflow \cite{li2020survey, wadden2019entity}. In practice, internship data collected from heterogeneous online sources still requires multiple additional steps before it can be used reliably, including field cleaning, schema normalization, duplicate handling, inconsistent metadata correction, and frontend-ready structuring.

\subsection{Complexity of Occupational Taxonomy Alignment}

Occupational taxonomies such as ESCO and O*NET provide valuable support for standardizing occupations, skills, and professional categories. They help connect informal recruitment language with more structured occupational representations. Nevertheless, these resources can be complex to integrate and maintain in a lightweight student-focused system, especially when the input data comes from noisy scraped sources, inconsistent JSON files, and employer-specific descriptions \cite{staab2009handbook}. Taxonomy alignment alone therefore does not fully solve the practical problem of preparing internship records for direct application use.

\subsection{Need for More Than Semantic Classification}

Lightweight semantic models, including sentence-transformer-based approaches, offer a practical way to improve the interpretation of noisy recruitment data without depending on very large generative systems. These models can support internship relevance classification and semantic enrichment by capturing meaning from titles and descriptions \cite{senger2024deep, zhang2024nnose}. However, semantic models alone are not sufficient. They still need to operate within a broader pipeline that includes deterministic cleaning, validation, normalization, skill extraction, domain categorization, and manual-review support for uncertain cases.

\subsection{Practical Limitations of LLM-Based Processing}

Large Language Models offer strong flexibility for interpreting noisy and ambiguous recruitment text. They can support classification, extraction, and summarization with limited task-specific engineering. However, relying on LLMs as the main processing method can introduce important deployment challenges, including financial cost, processing latency, external service dependency, privacy concerns, and reduced reproducibility \cite{bender2021dangers, schwartz2020green, menghani2023efficient}. These limitations are especially relevant when recruitment data must be processed continuously and at scale.

\subsection{Identified Research Gap}

Therefore, the gap identified in this thesis lies between the capabilities of existing approaches and the practical requirements of a lightweight internship-focused enrichment system. Existing solutions address parts of the problem, such as information extraction, taxonomy alignment, semantic classification, or recruitment data organization, but they often do not combine these elements into a local, low-cost, reproducible pipeline specifically designed for noisy internship and job posting data.


\section{Chapter Synthesis and Conclusion}

The literature reviewed in this chapter demonstrates that internship discovery is fundamentally
a semantic data-management problem involving heterogeneous data, vocabulary variation, and
retrieval complexity.
Research in information extraction, occupational taxonomies, semantic representation, and
hybrid retrieval collectively motivates the need for efficient approaches capable of balancing
semantic expressiveness with operational practicality.
These foundations support the thesis problem of semantic structuring for internship-oriented discovery.
