\chapter{Evidence-Based Evaluation of the Semantic Enrichment Pipeline}

\section{Chapter Introduction}

This chapter presents an evidence-based evaluation of the lightweight semantic enrichment pipeline
developed in this thesis. The purpose of the chapter is not to present unsupported benchmark superiority
claims, but to examine what the implemented evidence shows about noisy internship data, semantic
structuring, internship detection, structured discovery, uncertainty handling, and operational feasibility.
The chapter therefore treats technical performance measurements as feasibility evidence rather than as the
central research contribution.

The evaluation is aligned with the research questions defined in Chapter 1. RQ1 is addressed through the
observed noise and ambiguity in the inspected scraped-role sample. RQ2 is addressed through the
structured record outputs generated by the enrichment pipeline. RQ3 is addressed through frontend search,
filtering, and response evidence, while avoiding unsupported claims about formal user satisfaction. RQ4 is
addressed through human, ChatGPT5.5-assisted, and Transformer classification comparison. RQ5 is
addressed through operational measurements and explicit limitations.

This replacement chapter intentionally excludes unsupported research-ranking, extraction-precision, and
cost-comparison claims from the previous Chapter 6. Every numerical result included here is tied either to
the mandatory classification statistics supplied for this revision or to an identified proof file in the Chapter
6 evidence archive.

\subsection{Evaluation Sample}

The classification evaluation is conducted on a sample of 300 scraped roles. Human review is treated as
the reference interpretation for this sample. The selected classification evidence identifies 128 roles as real
internships, 9 as ambiguous cases, and 163 as fake or non-internship roles. This distribution shows that the
inspected data contains a substantial mixture of valid internships, uncertain opportunities, and irrelevant or
non-internship records.

The 300-role sample is therefore suitable for evaluating the thesis problem because it reflects the central
difficulty of internship aggregation: records collected from general recruitment sources cannot be assumed
to be genuine internships simply because they were retrieved by internship-oriented scraping or search
terms.

\subsection{Evaluation Boundaries}

The results in this chapter must be interpreted within several boundaries. First, the classification results are
sample-based and should not be generalized as universal model accuracy. Second, ambiguous cases
remain difficult to automate because they often depend on missing context, employer terminology, or
incomplete descriptions. Third, no formal user study was conducted; therefore, this chapter evaluates
structured discovery behavior rather than user satisfaction. Fourth, frontend timing results are local
measurements observed in the tested environment and should not be treated as universal cloud-deployment
guarantees. Fifth, files containing older or inconsistent classification distributions were excluded from the
quantitative argument to preserve a single coherent evidence basis.


\section{Dataset Noise and Internship-Posting Heterogeneity Results (RQ1)}

RQ1 asks what structural, lexical, and semantic inconsistencies characterize heterogeneous online
internship postings, and how these inconsistencies affect internship-oriented representation and discovery.
The 300-role classification sample directly supports this question by showing that scraped recruitment data
contains both genuine internships and many records that should not be accepted as internships.

In the selected sample, human inspection identified 128 real internships, 9 ambiguous cases, and 163 fake
or non-internship roles. The fake/non-internship group is larger than the accepted internship group, and the
ambiguous group indicates that some postings cannot be reliably resolved by simple keyword presence
alone. This supports the thesis claim that internship discovery is not only a storage or frontend problem; it
is also a semantic filtering and representation problem.

These results show that heterogeneous recruitment sources introduce uncertainty at the opportunity-type
level. A posting can contain internship-related words while still not being a real internship, and a posting
can be close to an internship but lack enough evidence for a confident automatic decision. Therefore, a
thesis evaluation centered only on frontend display or raw scraping volume would be incomplete. The first
empirical finding is that the data itself requires classification, normalization, and uncertainty-aware
treatment before being used for internship-oriented discovery.

indicators. This supports the thesis design choice of combining automatic enrichment with review-aware
metadata rather than forcing every posting into a fully confident decision.


\section{Internship Detection Evaluation Against Human Review (RQ4 and RQ2)}

This section evaluates internship detection against human review. It supports RQ2 by examining the
classification component within the enrichment model and supports RQ4 by showing how ambiguous
cases affect automated decision-making.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{llllllll}
        \hline
        \textbf{System} & \textbf{Total Roles} & \textbf{Real Internships} & \textbf{Ambiguous Cases} &
        \textbf{Fake / Non-Internships} & \textbf{Agreement / Accuracy vs Human} &
        \textbf{Precision} & \textbf{Recall} \\
        \hline
        Human review & 300 & 128 & 9 & 163 & Reference & Reference & Reference \\
        ChatGPT5.5-assisted review & 300 & 128 & 9 & 163 & 100\% sample agreement & 100\% & 100\% \\
        Transformer & 300 & 132 & 0 & 168 & 95.19\% & 93.85\% & 95.31\% \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Internship classification comparison against human review}
    \label{tab:internship-classification-comparison}
\end{table}

Human review is the reference for this evaluation. On the inspected 300-role sample, human review
identified 128 real internships, 9 ambiguous cases, and 163 fake or non-internship roles.

ChatGPT5.5-assisted review produced the same final distribution as the human inspection. This is reported
as 100\% sample-level agreement with the human review on this specific 300-role sample. It must not be
interpreted as proof that ChatGPT5.5 is universally 100\% accurate on all internship classification tasks.

The Transformer classified 132 roles as real internships and 168 roles as fake or non-internships. Unlike
the human and ChatGPT5.5-assisted review, the Transformer does not produce an ambiguous category.
Therefore, its performance is interpreted as binary real/fake internship detection. Against the human clear
real/fake cases, the Transformer achieved 95.19\% accuracy, 93.85\% precision, and 95.31\% recall.

Precision measures how trustworthy the system is when it classifies a role as a real internship. A high
precision score means that fewer fake or non-internship roles are incorrectly accepted as real internships.
Recall measures how many real internships the system successfully finds. A high recall score means that
fewer genuine internships are missed.

These results support a cautious interpretation. The Transformer is effective as a binary detector for real
internship identification in this inspected sample, but its lack of an ambiguous output limits its ability to
represent uncertainty. Ambiguous cases therefore remain better suited to human review or
ChatGPT5.5-assisted review, especially when the posting contains incomplete evidence or
employer-specific terminology.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity (RQ5)}

Several limitations affect the interpretation of the findings. First, the classification evaluation is based on a
300-role inspected sample. Although useful for analyzing noisy scraped recruitment data, this sample
cannot represent every platform, country, domain, or language context. Second, the classification table is
treated as the selected evidence basis for this rewritten chapter; proof files containing older or inconsistent
distributions were excluded rather than mixed with it.

Third, ambiguous cases remain a core limitation. The Transformer does not produce an ambiguous
category, which means it cannot directly represent uncertainty in the same way as human or
ChatGPT5.5-assisted review. Fourth, ChatGPT5.5-assisted review is reported as sample-level agreement
with human review, not universal correctness. Fifth, the frontend timing results were produced under local
test conditions and can be affected by hardware, cache state, software configuration, and deployment
environment.

Sixth, no formal user study was conducted. Therefore, this chapter avoids claims about user satisfaction or
real student usability. The evidence supports structured discovery behavior, metadata coverage, and local
frontend responsiveness, but it does not establish how students would perceive or use the system in a
controlled user-evaluation setting. Seventh, the evidence is limited to the proof files available for this
chapter. Unsupported old benchmark metrics and derived claims without a traceable proof file were not
included.

\section{Chapter Conclusion}

This chapter replaced unsupported benchmark-oriented claims with an evidence-based evaluation of the semantic enrichment pipeline. The 300-role classification sample demonstrates that scraped recruitment data contains a meaningful mixture of real internships, fake or non-internship roles, and ambiguous cases. The human, ChatGPT5.5-assisted, and Transformer comparison shows strong sample-level classification behavior while also demonstrating why ambiguity must be preserved rather than hidden.

The enriched frontend catalog evidence shows that the pipeline produces structured internship records with internship probabilities, extracted skills, salary fields where available, links, descriptions, and manual-review indicators. The frontend search/filter and response measurements show that the enriched records can support fast structured discovery under local production test conditions. The build and file-size evidence further supports the operational feasibility of the lightweight approach within the evaluated scope.

Overall, the evidence supports the thesis argument that lightweight semantic enrichment can transform noisy scraped recruitment data into structured, interpretable, and discovery-ready internship records. The findings remain bounded by the 300-role sample, the local test environment, the absence of a formal user study, and the limited scope of the available proof files. These limitations do not invalidate the results, but they define the conditions under which the results should be defended.
