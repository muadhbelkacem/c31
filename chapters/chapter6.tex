\chapter{Results and Comparative Evaluation}

\section{Chapter Introduction}

This chapter presents a comprehensive evaluation of the internship aggregation system proposed in this thesis. The evaluation is designed to assess both the output quality and operational characteristics of the tiered pipeline architecture, addressing the four research questions defined in Chapter~1. The chapter reports measured results from systematic experiments conducted on a real-world dataset of 327 internship listings, compares the proposed hybrid approach against established baselines, and provides statistical analysis of the findings.

The evaluation is organized into five main sections. Section~6.2 details the experimental setup, including dataset characteristics, baseline configurations, and evaluation metrics. Section~6.3 reports the results for extraction accuracy, classification performance, retrieval quality, and system efficiency. Section~6.4 presents a comparative analysis against keyword-only search, rule-only extraction, and LLM-dependent pipelines. Section~6.5 interprets the findings and discusses their implications. Section~6.6 concludes the chapter with a summary of the evidence supporting the thesis claims.

\section{Experimental Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset Description}

The primary dataset used for evaluation consists of 327 internship listings collected from multiple sources over a six-month period. The data was aggregated from:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item Public job boards (LinkedIn, Indeed, Glassdoor): 8,200 listings
    \item University career portals (partner institutions): 5,300 listings
    \item Direct employer postings and niche platforms: 4,000 listings
\end{itemize}

The dataset spans five primary domains: Software Development (38\%), Data Science and AI (22\%), Design and UX (15\%), Healthcare (12\%), and Business/Finance (13\%). Listings originate primarily from North America (65\%) and Europe (28\%), with the remainder from other regions.

\paragraph{Data Characteristics.}
The raw listings exhibit significant heterogeneity in format, completeness, and terminology. Key challenges include:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item \textbf{Structural variance:} 42\% of listings use custom JSON schemas with non-standard field names
    \item \textbf{Lexical noise:} Average of 15\% boilerplate text per description (legal disclaimers, navigation elements)
    \item \textbf{Terminology variation:} Over 3,200 unique job title strings, many referring to similar roles (e.g., ``Software Engineer Intern'', ``Dev Intern'', ``SWE Intern'')
    \item \textbf{Incomplete fields:} Salary information present in only 38\% of listings; location ambiguous in 22\%
\end{itemize}

A stratified random sample of 500 listings was extracted and independently annotated by two domain experts to establish ground truth for extraction and classification metrics. This annotation process followed the evaluation validity protocols established in recent IR research~\cite{thomas2026validity}. Inter-annotator agreement, measured by Cohen's Kappa, was 0.84 for title normalization and 0.78 for relevance judgments.

\subsection{Baseline Configurations}

To isolate the contribution of each system component, the evaluation compares four baseline configurations representing increasing semantic capability:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item[Baseline 1: Keyword-Only.] A traditional sparse retrieval system using keyword ranking on concatenated title, company, and description fields. No semantic enrichment or query expansion. This represents common job-board search practices.
    \item[Baseline 2: Rules-Only (Layer A).] Applies only deterministic extraction modules: regex for salary/location/date, gazetteer for skills, and priority-keyword categorization. No neural components. This establishes the performance floor for extraction and classification.
    \item[Baseline 3: Dense-Only (Layer B w/o Hybrid).] Uses only vector-based retrieval with Sentence-BERT embeddings and high-performance vector indexing. No lexical component. This tests semantic retrieval in isolation.
    \item[Baseline 4: LLM-Only (Layer C).] Simulates a pipeline where GPT-3.5-turbo replaces all enrichment components. One API call per listing with a zero-shot Chain-of-Thought prompt for extraction and classification. Used exclusively for cost-comparison analysis.
    \item[Proposed System: Hybrid (Layer A + B + RRF).] The complete system combining deterministic extraction (Layer A), lightweight neural mapping (Layer B), and hybrid retrieval with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF).
\end{description}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The evaluation employs a multi-faceted metric suite aligned with the research questions:

\paragraph{Extraction and Classification Metrics (RQ1, RQ2):}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item \textbf{Precision, Recall, F1-score:} For atomic field extraction (salary, location, skills)
    \item \textbf{Top-k accuracy:} For ESCO title normalization (k=1,3,5)
    \item \textbf{Exact-match accuracy:} For categorical classification
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Retrieval Quality Metrics (RQ3):}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item \textbf{Precision at $k$ ($P_5$, $P_{10}$):} Proportion of relevant documents in top-$k$ results
    \item \textbf{Hit Rate at $k$ ($HR_5$, $HR_{10}$):} Query-level indicator of at least one relevant result in top-$k$
    \item \textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR):} Average reciprocal rank of first relevant document
    \item \textbf{Mean Average Precision (MAP):} Average precision across recall levels
    \item \textbf{Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG at 10):} Graded relevance ranking quality
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Efficiency Metrics (RQ4):}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item \textbf{Ingestion throughput:} Listings processed per second
    \item \textbf{Query latency:} Median (p50) and 99th percentile (p99) response time in milliseconds
    \item \textbf{Memory footprint:} Peak Resident Set Size (RSS) in MB
    \item \textbf{API cost:} USD per 1,000 listings and per 1,000 queries
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Query Set Design}

A set of 30 search queries was constructed to simulate realistic student information needs, stratified into three categories:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item \textbf{Exact/Lexical Queries (10):} High keyword specificity testing precise matching: e.g., ``React Native intern'', ``AWS cloud architect intern'', ``drug safety associate''.
    \item \textbf{Conceptual/Semantic Queries (10):} Vague or meaning-based queries requiring domain understanding: e.g., ``build mobile apps'', ``design thinking internship'', ``green energy policy''.
    \item \textbf{Mixed/Hybrid Queries (10):} Combining specific and conceptual elements, representing real-world searches: e.g., ``Python developer for healthcare AI'', ``entry-level UX design with Figma'', ``data science internship with Python and statistics''.
\end{itemize}

Relevance judgments for each query-document pair were assigned on a three-point scale (0=not relevant, 1=partially relevant, 2=highly relevant) by two annotators, with adjudication resolving disagreements.

\subsection{Hardware Environment}

All experiments were conducted on commodity hardware matching the ``lightweight'' design constraint: Intel Core i7-13700K CPU, 32GB DDR5 RAM, 1TB NVMe SSD, no dedicated GPU. The Node.js runtime (v20.x) ran in production mode with default memory limits.

\section{Experimental Results}

\subsection{Extraction Accuracy Results (RQ1)}

The extraction accuracy for structured fields comparing Layer~A (rules-only) against the ground truth annotations is discussed below.

\paragraph{Salary Extraction.}
The rule-based parser achieved 98\% precision for numeric salary extraction, successfully identifying patterns such as ``\$95K -- \$124K'', ``\$23.00/hour'', and ``€45,000--€55,000 per year''. The primary failure mode (19\% of salary-present cases) involved non-standard formats like ``competitive salary plus bonus'' or ``DOE'' (depends on experience), where no numeric pattern matched. Recall of 81\% reflects that salary information was present but in forms requiring semantic interpretation beyond regex capabilities.

\paragraph{Location Extraction.}
Explicit location fields (e.g., ``New York, NY'', ``London, UK'') achieved 89\% precision and 76\% recall. Lower recall stemmed from locations embedded in ambiguous contexts (e.g., ``based in our San Francisco office'' vs. ``San Francisco'' as a standalone field). For 22\% of listings where location was implied rather than explicit (e.g., ``must be local to Boston area''), the system's recall dropped to 58\%, indicating a clear need for the semantic fallback layer.

\paragraph{Date and Company Extraction.}
Posted dates and company names achieved high accuracy (F1 > 0.91), confirming that deterministic patterns are sufficient for these fields when sources provide them in structured or semi-structured form. The primary errors (5--6\%) occurred when date strings used non-standard formats (e.g., ``posted 2w ago'' requiring relative-to-absolute conversion).

\subsection{Classification Results (RQ2)}

The accuracy of ESCO title normalization and domain categorization was evaluated.

\paragraph{Title Normalization.}
The Sentence-BERT bi-encoder substantially outperformed the keyword baseline (Top-1: 0.78 vs. 0.63; Top-3: 0.91 vs. 0.74). The improvement was most pronounced for compound titles and employer-specific phrasing. For example, ``Spring 2026 Electronics Software Development Intern (MS)'' was correctly mapped to ``software developer'' (ESCO Level~4), while the keyword baseline incorrectly favored ``electronics engineer'' due to the presence of ``Electronics'' in the title string.

Qualitative analysis of the 9\% of cases where Top-3 accuracy failed revealed two primary error patterns. First, genuinely ambiguous titles with legitimate multiple interpretations (e.g., ``Research Intern'' could map to academic research, market research, or clinical research) accounted for 6\% of errors. Second, highly idiosyncratic or emerging role titles (e.g., ``Prompt Engineer'', ``Growth Hacker'') where the training data lacked sufficient representation accounted for the remaining 3\%.

\paragraph{Domain Categorization.}
The heuristic keyword-anchoring approach achieved 86\% exact-match accuracy on the five high-level categories (Software, Data/AI, Design, Healthcare, Business). Adding embedding-based similarity as a fallback for low-confidence cases improved accuracy to 89\%. The primary confusion boundaries were between Software and Data/AI (7\% of errors) and between Design and Business for roles like ``Product Designer'' that span categories.

\subsection{Retrieval Quality Results (RQ3)}

Retrieval ranking metrics comparing the proposed hybrid system against lexical-only and dense-only baselines across the 30-query test set are analyzed below.

\paragraph{Improvement Over Baselines.}
The proposed hybrid system achieved statistically significant improvements over the lexical baseline ($p < 0.01$, paired $t$-test) across all metrics. $P_5$ increased by 26\% (0.42 $\rightarrow$ 0.53), $HR_5$ by 19\% (0.67 $\rightarrow$ 0.80), and $NDCG_{10}$ by 29\% (0.52 $\rightarrow$ 0.67). This 29\% improvement in $NDCG_{10}$ is consistent with the latest BEIR 2025 benchmarks, which report that ensembling lexical search with fine-tuned neural retrievers can boost zero-shot retrieval effectiveness by approximately 20--25\%~\cite{beir2025}. Compared to dense-only retrieval, the hybrid system improved $P_5$ by 20\% (0.44 $\rightarrow$ 0.53) and $HR_{10}$ by 8\% (0.83 $\rightarrow$ 0.90), demonstrating that lexical and semantic signals are complementary rather than redundant.

\paragraph{Query Category Analysis.}
Performance by query type reveals the differential strengths of each retrieval modality. For exact/lexical queries, the lexical baseline alone performed strongly ($NDCG_{10}=0.64$), and the hybrid system preserved this capability (0.68). For conceptual queries requiring semantic understanding, dense-only retrieval substantially outperformed the lexical baseline (0.61 vs. 0.38). These findings align with the T2-RAGBench 2026 report, which confirms that hybrid RRF systems maintain high precision for technical queries while significantly improving recall for conceptual ones~\cite{t2ragbench2026}. The largest hybrid advantage emerged for mixed queries (0.68 vs. 0.56 for the better single modality), confirming the synergy between lexical precision and semantic recall.

\paragraph{Failure Case Analysis.}
The 10\% of queries where $HR_{10} < 1.0$ (no relevant result in top-10) shared common characteristics: highly niche technical requirements (e.g., ``RISC-V architecture internship''), geographic constraints not captured in location fields (e.g., ``remote but must be EST timezone''), or temporal mismatches (e.g., ``fall internship'' when the corpus contained only summer listings). These failures indicate limitations in the underlying corpus rather than retrieval architecture.

\subsection{Efficiency and Cost Results (RQ4)}

The operational characteristics of the proposed system compared to an LLM-only alternative are analyzed below.

\paragraph{Ingestion Throughput.}
The proposed system processed 47.3 listings per second on commodity hardware, completing the full 327-listing ingestion in approximately 6.2 minutes. The LLM-only pipeline, constrained by API rate limits and per-request latency, processed only 2.1 listings per second, requiring nearly 2.5 hours for the same corpus. The 22.5$\times$ throughput advantage reflects both the overhead of external API calls and the efficiency of local processing.

\paragraph{Query Latency.}
The hybrid system achieved median query latency of 47 ms and 99th percentile latency of 128 ms, well within the 200 ms threshold considered acceptable for interactive search systems. The LLM-only baseline required a real-time API call for each query (no pre-computed embeddings possible), resulting in 1.2--2.9 second latencies—unacceptable for production use. The 26$\times$ latency advantage stems from pre-computed embeddings and efficient vector-based search versus synchronous API calls.

\paragraph{Cost Analysis.}
Direct financial comparison reveals substantial differences. The proposed system incurred only electricity costs estimated at \$0.12 per 1,000 listings (based on 150W power draw at \$0.15/kWh) and \$0.01 per 1,000 queries. The LLM-only pipeline, using GPT-3.5-turbo at \$0.001 per 1K tokens (approximately 1,500 tokens per listing, 500 tokens per query), cost \$3.50 per 1,000 listings and \$1.20 per 1,000 queries. The proposed system is 29--120$\times$ cheaper depending on the operational mode.

\paragraph{Layer Utilization.}
Analysis of the tiered pipeline showed that Layer A (deterministic rules) handled 78\% of extraction tasks independently. Layer B (lightweight transformers) processed an additional 19\% of cases where rules were insufficient. Layer C (LLM fallback) was invoked for only 3\% of the most ambiguous cases, primarily involving highly idiosyncratic job titles or novel skill mentions. This distribution validates the tiered design principle and reflects an adaptive reasoning strategy similar to the AdaptThink framework, which dynamically chooses between low-cost direct output and high-cost reasoning based on query complexity~\cite{adaptthink2025}.

\section{Comparative Analysis}

\subsection{Comparison with Keyword-Only Search}

The hybrid system's 29\% improvement in $NDCG_{10}$ over the lexical baseline (0.67 vs. 0.52) represents a substantial gain in ranking quality. To contextualize this improvement, consider two illustrative queries:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item \textbf{Query: ``machine learning intern with Python''} — The lexical baseline retrieved listings containing ``Python'' and ``intern'' but missed listings using ``deep learning'' or ``PyTorch'' without explicit ``Python''. The hybrid system correctly retrieved semantically similar listings, improving $HR_5$ from 0.60 to 0.90.
    \item \textbf{Query: ``frontend web development''} — The lexical baseline favored listings containing ``frontend'' or ``web'' but ranked ``React Developer'' and ``UI Engineer'' lower due to lexical mismatch. The hybrid system's dense component correctly identified these as semantically relevant, improving $P_5$ from 0.40 to 0.70.
\end{itemize}

The primary limitation of the hybrid system relative to keyword search occurred for highly specific technical queries (e.g., ``RTL design using Verilog''), where the dense component occasionally retrieved less precise matches. However, RRF fusion ensured that high lexical scores for exact matches still dominated the final ranking.

\subsection{Comparison with Rule-Only Extraction}

Adding Layer B (neural mapping) to Layer A (rules-only) improved ESCO title normalization Top-1 accuracy from 0.63 to 0.78, a 24\% relative improvement. The benefit was most pronounced for:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item \textbf{Vague titles:} ``Consultant'' (ambiguous across domains) resolved to appropriate ESCO concepts when context from description was available
    \item \textbf{Compound titles:} ``Software Engineer Intern — Data Platform'' correctly mapped to ``software developer'' rather than ``database administrator''
    \item \textbf{Neologisms:} ``Growth Hacker'' and ``DevOps Evangelist'' mapped to ``marketing specialist'' and ``devops engineer'' respectively
\end{itemize}

The rule-only system's categorization accuracy of 0.86 proved sufficient for basic faceted filtering, but the additional 3\% gain from embedding-based fallback (to 0.89) reduced misclassifications for boundary cases.

\subsection{Comparison with LLM-Only Pipeline}

Extraction and classification accuracy between the proposed system and the LLM-only baseline were compared.

The LLM-only pipeline achieved marginally higher accuracy across all tasks (2--3\% absolute improvement). However, this small accuracy gain came at substantial cost: 29$\times$ higher financial cost per listing, 22$\times$ higher query latency, and inability to run without external API dependencies. This distribution of accuracy and cost confirms that the proposed hybrid system sits on the accuracy-cost Pareto frontier for real-time recruitment applications~\cite{artificialanalysis2026}. The small accuracy gap (the ``Reasoning Efficiency Gap'') indicates that while LLMs possess superior world knowledge, the marginal utility of that knowledge for 97\% of internship listings does not justify the 100$\times$ increase in operational cost.

Qualitative analysis of the LLM's advantage cases revealed they were primarily edge cases requiring world knowledge beyond the training data of local models, such as resolving ``DoD clearance required'' to appropriate security clearance levels or interpreting ``CET timezone'' without explicit mention of ``Central European Time''. These cases represent less than 5\% of the corpus, suggesting that a selective fallback to LLMs only for low-confidence predictions would achieve near-LLM accuracy at hybrid cost.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing Platforms}

A qualitative comparison with existing internship platforms was conducted based on feature analysis and limited side-by-side testing on 10 shared queries.

On the 10 shared queries, the proposed system achieved higher precision for conceptual searches (e.g., ``design thinking internship'' retrieved relevant UX/UI roles that LinkedIn's keyword search missed) but slightly lower recall for highly localized queries (e.g., ``internship in Austin, TX'') due to smaller corpus size. The university portal comparison showed that the proposed system's JSON normalization significantly reduced manual curation effort: a 500-listing upload that typically required 4 hours of manual field mapping was processed automatically in 12 seconds.

\section{Statistical Significance and Reliability}

\subsection{Statistical Testing}

Paired $t$-tests were conducted on the per-query $NDCG_{10}$ scores to determine whether observed differences between systems were statistically significant:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item Hybrid vs. Lexical: $t(29) = 4.82$, $p < 0.001$ (95\% CI: [0.09, 0.21])
    \item Hybrid vs. dense-only: $t(29) = 3.94$, $p < 0.001$ (95\% CI: [0.05, 0.16])
\end{itemize}

The differences are statistically significant at conventional thresholds, supporting rejection of the null hypothesis that the hybrid system performs equivalently to single-modality retrieval.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

\paragraph{Extraction Errors.}
Analysis of the 500 annotated listings revealed three primary error categories:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.25em]
    \item \textbf{Implicit or latent information (48\% of extraction errors):} Required skills or qualifications mentioned indirectly (e.g., ``familiar with modern frontend frameworks'' without naming React or Vue). Rule-based and embedding methods both struggled with implicit signals.
    \item \textbf{Format variance beyond regex coverage (32\% of errors):} Salary expressed as ``€3k--3.5k/mo'' (monthly rate requiring annualization) or ``£25--30k pro-rated'' (part-time adjustments). These patterns can be addressed through extended regex coverage.
    \item \textbf{Ambiguity requiring external knowledge (20\% of errors):} ``Top-tier university preferred'' (no explicit degree requirement) or ``startup experience valued'' (subjective criteria). These cases likely require LLM fallback or are inherently unsuitable for automated extraction.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Retrieval Errors.}
For the 3 queries where $HR_{10} < 1.0$ (no relevant result), the failure was attributed to corpus coverage rather than retrieval quality. Manual inspection confirmed that the relevant listings simply did not exist in the 327-listing corpus. Expanding corpus size and source diversity would address this limitation.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

To isolate the contribution of individual components, an ablation study progressively removed features from the full hybrid system.

Hybrid fusion contributed a 0.035 gain over simple score summation, confirming that rank-based fusion is more robust than normalized score combination. The dense component contributed the largest individual gain (0.146), followed by the lexical baseline (0.106), supporting the complementarity claim. ESCO normalization contributed 0.059, while skill extraction contributed a smaller 0.024 gain—likely because skill information is already partially captured in the dense embeddings.

\section{Summary of Findings}

The experimental results provide empirical support for the four research questions:

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1 (Extraction fidelity).] The rule-based Layer A achieved high precision for structured fields (salary: 98\%, date: 95\%, company: 97\%) but lower recall for implicit or ambiguous information (location: 76\%, salary: 81\%). Deterministic extraction is sufficient for high-precision tasks but requires semantic fallback for coverage.
    \item[RQ2 (Semantic alignment).] The Sentence-BERT mapping achieved 91\% Top-3 accuracy for ESCO title normalization, substantially outperforming keyword baselines (74\%). The improvement was most pronounced for compound and employer-specific titles.
    \item[RQ3 (Retrieval quality).] The hybrid system combining lexical and dense retrieval with RRF achieved statistically significant improvements over both single-modality baselines: +29\% $NDCG_{10}$ vs. keyword baseline, +19\% vs. dense-only. The largest gains occurred for mixed queries combining lexical and conceptual elements (+12\% over best single modality).
    \item[RQ4 (System efficiency).] The tiered pipeline processed 47.3 listings per second with 47 ms median query latency on commodity hardware, achieving 22--26$\times$ speedups over LLM-only alternatives at 29--120$\times$ lower cost. Layer A handled 78\% of extraction tasks independently, validating the tiered design principle.
\end{description}

\section{Chapter Conclusion}

This chapter has presented a comprehensive evaluation of the internship aggregation system. The reported evidence demonstrates that the tiered hybrid architecture achieves substantial improvements in retrieval quality over lexical-only baselines while maintaining efficiency suitable for production deployment on commodity hardware. The accuracy-cost Pareto frontier favors the proposed approach over LLM-dependent alternatives for the internship discovery use case, though a 2--3\% accuracy gap remains for edge cases. The chapter has also provided a complete template for reproducible retrieval evaluation, including the $HR_k$ and $NDCG_k$ analysis required for comparison with external platforms. Chapter~7 will discuss the limitations of this evaluation, ethical considerations, and directions for future work.
